Castrum Maris

Crusader coastal fortress · Levantine littoral · c. 1190–1250
Concentric enceinte on a limestone headland · 54 m above mean sea level

ON THE RECONSTRUCTION

No single castle is copied here. The fortress is assembled from documented Frankish practice in the Latin East, so that every element shown has a real precedent.

Precedents

Krak des Chevaliers (Hospitaller, Syria) — the concentric plan, the round mural towers and the colossal battered talus of the inner ward.
Saône / Qal'at Salah ed-Din — the rock-cut ditch with a freestanding needle of living rock left standing to carry the bridge.
Château Pèlerin (Atlit, Templar, 1218) — a fortress thrown across a headland, defended landward and open to resupply by sea.
Tortosa & Byblos — antique granite column shafts re-laid as through-binders, their sawn ends still showing in the wall face.

Why it is built this way

The talus is not decoration. A battered plinth denies a ram a square face, forces a mine gallery deeper and longer, and turns anything dropped from the parapet into a ricochet that skips outward into the attacking ranks.

Towers are round on the exposed faces because a corner is where a wall is quarried away, stone by stone, by miners working under a penthouse. A curve has no corner, and it sheds the stone-thrower's shot instead of taking it square.

The entrance bends. A straight passage can be rushed; an axis that turns inside the gate breaks the charge, keeps a ram from ever reaching the second door, and leaves the attacker in a killing box under the murder holes with his shield on the wrong arm.

The cistern matters more than the walls. A Frankish garrison held out on stored rainwater, not on its masonry — siege after siege ended at the bottom of a dry cistern.

The burgus and the ancient city

A castle of this size was an economy as much as a garrison. Outside the ditch stands the burgus — a street of flat-roofed houses with outside stairs to their roofs, a parish church apsed to the east like the castle chapel, a market and walled garden plots. The settlement lived off the castle and paid for it.

Further inland lies the colonnade the masons robbed. Frankish builders treated antiquity as a quarry: the granite shafts running through the sea wall as binders came off a street like that one, and the dressed blocks went into the curtain.

Materials & measures

Curtain 3.4 m thick, 16 m to the wall-walk · parapet 2.0 m · merlons 1.30 × 1.90 m at 2.40 m centres · talus batter 1 : 0.45 · round towers Ø 11 m rising 21 m · donjon 21 × 18 m, 29 m · ditch 26 m wide, 20 m deep.
Masonry is drafted-margin ashlar with a rusticated boss, the standard Frankish dressing, laid in 0.80 m courses over a rubble-and-mortar core. Colour varies by build: the keep and the gate took the best stone on the site, the curtain is greyer, and the outbuildings are coursed rubble.

Flora

Aleppo pine, Italian cypress, olive on drystone terraces, tamarisk at the strand, and a garrigue of thyme, sage and rockrose — the Levantine coastal assemblage of the period. Nothing post-Columbian is planted here: no prickly pear, no agave, no eucalyptus.
